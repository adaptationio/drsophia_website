Document deep-dives

For each document the four sub-questions are: what is it, does it bind us, what does it require of a CDSS / ambient scribe, and what do we already do vs. what do we need to add.

1. SANC Code of Ethics for Nursing Practitioners in South Africa (2013, current)

What it is. A 9-page binding code under the Nursing Act 33/2005, published and enforced by the South African Nursing Council. Verbatim from §1 Preamble: "The Code of Ethics does not only provide guidance to nurses in the process of their ethical decision-making, but is a binding document the content of which must be complied with." §5.3 explicitly: "Failure to adhere to the provisions of the Code or violations thereof, attracts the same sanctions as those of non-adherence to the regulations/rules regarding acts and omissions and may bring the Nursing Practitioner's fitness to practice into question and endanger his/her registration to practice."

Does it bind us? Indirectly — it binds the nurses using ROSE/Sophia at the Sha'p Left clinics, not Botaniqal as a vendor. But it sets the floor for what the system must support: confidentiality, informed consent, documentation, advocacy, non-maleficence, accountability for omissions.

Key requirements relevant to a CDSS: - Veracity + Fidelity — nurses are required to provide accurate, truthful information to the patient and to keep balanced, factual, confidential records. Implication: Sophia must never fabricate or paraphrase findings; quote-grounding (already a Sophia property) is load-bearing. - Autonomy — "Respect for the autonomy of eligible persons (healthcare users) to make their own decisions and choices." Implication: ROSE recommendations must be advisory and overridable by the nurse; consent must be an explicit gate, not a checkbox; there must be a way for the patient to refuse without the system blocking the consult. - Confidentiality + privacy (§4 Value Statement) — informs §5.1.2 (Healthcare Users) + §5.1.3 (Colleagues). Implication: per-clinic and per-nurse access scoping; no cross-clinic readability without explicit consent; no analytics that re-identify. - Accountability (§3 last clause): "As professionals, Nursing Practitioners will be personally accountable for all actions and omissions while carrying out their responsibilities in their profession and must always be able to justify all decisions taken and carried out." Implication: every CDSS-suggested action that the nurse accepts must be attributable to the nurse, not to the system, in the audit log; every override must be capturable with a free-text reason.

What we already do vs. need to add. Sophia's existing audit log + scope-aware suppression covers attribution. Override-with-reason is implemented for prescribing in the AU build but not in the SA Tier 1 design — needs to be in the Sha'p Left MVP from day one.

2. National Health Act Regulations: Categories of Hospitals (GG R.185, 2 March 2012)

What it is. A 30-page Government Gazette regulation under §35 + §90 of the National Health Act 61/2003, defining the five categories of public hospital (district / regional / tertiary / central / specialised) and their bed-band, service-mix, and referral relationships. Includes a complete annex-list of every public hospital in the country grouped by category, district and bed count.

Does it bind us? Sha'p Left is not a hospital; Sha'p Left units are nurse-led PHC clinics (sitting below district-hospital level in the referral pyramid). The regulation defines the upward referral pathway that ROSE has to know about: a Sha'p Left nurse referring a patient escalates to the district hospital that serves the relevant health district + sub-district. Verbatim §3(2)(c): a district hospital must "have general practitioners and clinical nurse practitioners primary health services" — the same regulation that recognises clinical nurse practitioners (CNPs / PCNS) at the district hospital recognises them as the cohort Sha'p Left employs.

Key requirements relevant to a CDSS: - ROSE's referral logic must select the correct district hospital for the patient's geography from the public-hospitals annex (32 KB list, structured by province → district → sub-district → facility name → bed count → category). For demo: hard-code 11 referral destinations matching the 11 current Sha'p Left clinics. For production: load the gazette annex into reference data. - Specialist services available at district level are only paediatrics, OB-Gyn, internal medicine, general surgery, family physician (§3(4)). ROSE must not suggest a referral to e.g. cardiology unless escalating directly to a regional hospital. This is a "scope of referral" guard. - The regulation does not address private hospitals beyond categorising them as "for profit" or "not for profit" (§9). Private specialists in the patient's geography are out of scope for the public-pyramid path but might be available to insured patients — a Tier 4 expansion question.

What we already do vs. need to add. No SA referral logic exists in Sophia today (AU-only). Tier 1 needs a minimum: free-text "refer to district hospital" with the patient's home district auto-populated from clinic geography. Full annex ingestion is a Tier 2 task.

3. HPCSA Booklet 9: Guidelines on the Keeping of Patient Records (2016, Nov 2022 revision)

What it is. The HPCSA's binding ethical-rule booklet covering clinical record content, signing, alteration, retention, ownership, access. Title-page note: "applicable to health care practitioners in private practice (including managed health care organisations), as well as to those in the employ of the public service."

Does it bind us? Indirectly via the practitioners using ROSE — but the rules cascade hard onto any vendor whose system holds clinical records.

Key verbatim requirements (verbatim where load-bearing): - §4.1 — "Health care practitioners should enter and maintain at least the following information for each patient consulted: 4.1.1 Personal (identifying) particulars of the patient; 4.1.2 The bio-psychosocial history of the patient, including allergies and idiosyncrasies; 4.1.3 The time, date and place of every consultation; 4.1.4 The assessment of the patient's condition; 4.1.5 The proposed clinical management of the patient; 4.1.6 The medication and dosage prescribed; 4.1.7 Details of referrals to specialists, if any; 4.1.8 The patient's reaction to treatment or medication, including adverse effects; 4.1.9 Test results; 4.1.10 Imaging investigation results; 4.1.11 Information on the times that the patient was booked off from work and the relevant reasons; 4.1.12 Written proof of informed consent, where applicable." - §4.2 — "Records should be kept in non-erasable ink and erasure fluid should not be used." The digital equivalent is immutable record + visible audit trail of alterations (compare §8: "No information or entry may be removed from a health record. An error or incorrect entry discovered in the record may be corrected by placing a line through it with ink and correcting it. The date of change must be entered and the correction must be signed in full."). - §9 — Retention: 6 years for adults from "dormant" date; until 21st birthday for minors; lifetime for mentally incompetent patients; 20 years for occupational health records (OHSA 85/1993); at least 25 years recommended for conditions that may have long latency (e.g. asbestosis); destruction in provincial hospitals only on Deputy DG authorisation. - §10 — Ownership rules in private practice; on practice closure or practitioner death, records must be transferred to a successor or kept in safe-keeping by an identified custodian for ≥12 months. - §11 — Access: 12+ year-olds may directly access their own records; under 16s require written authorisation by the patient before parent/guardian access; termination-of-pregnancy records cannot be disclosed to anyone but the patient regardless of age (Choice on Termination of Pregnancy Act 92/1996); third-party disclosure only with written authorisation, court order, or serious public-health threat (NHA §61(2003)). - §12 — CD-ROM / digital storage allowed if: write-once media, encrypted, password-protected, off-site backup copy, "effective safeguards against unauthorised use or retransmission". - §13 Checklist — "Records should be complete, but concise. Records should be consistent. Self-serving or disapproving comments should be avoided. … A standardised format should be used (e.g. notes should contain in order the history, physical findings, investigations, diagnosis, treatment and outcome.)."

Implications for a CDSS / ambient scribe: - Mandatory record schema — every consult must capture all 12 fields in §4.1 by the time the encounter is closed. Sophia's existing SOAPIE/SOAP schema covers most; bio-psychosocial history needs to be a first-class first-encounter object that persists across visits. - Immutable + auditable corrections — digital equivalent of the "line through with date and signature". Sophia's existing audit log already supports this; we need to surface the audit log on the patient record view at the SA Tier 1. - Retention engine — 6/21/lifetime/20/25-year rules need to be encoded in the data lifecycle policy. AWS S3 with object-lock and Glacier transitions can cover this; out of scope for the demo, in scope for production. - Encryption + 2-site backup is mandatory, not optional. Sophia already meets this on AWS ap-southeast-2; for SA the destination would be af-south-1 (Cape Town) once the data-residency migration is on the roadmap.

4. HPCSA Booklet 10: Ethical Guidelines for Good Practice in Telehealth (December 2021, revised)

What it is. The HPCSA's binding ethical-rule booklet on telehealth (formerly "telemedicine"). Renamed in 2021 to be "more inclusive" — covers all HPCSA-registered professions (doctors, dentists, psychologists, dieticians, etc.). Replaces the COVID-era 2020 amendments; restores most of the pre-COVID restrictions but loosens some.

Does it bind us? Yes, materially. Sha'p Left consults are face-to-face (nurse + patient in clinic), so the bulk of telehealth rules don't apply directly to the consult. But any time ROSE escalates to a remote doctor (Tier 4 supervising prescribing authority — see Cipla MoU Article 2 scope tier), and any time a nurse uses Sophia/ROSE remotely (e.g. reviewing records from outside the clinic), telehealth rules apply.

Key verbatim requirements: - §3.7 — "Telehealth is intended to be utilised to replicate physical consultations as far as possible, but not as a substitute. It is desirable that the practitioner shall have established professional relationship with their patient before Telehealth services can be considered, although, this is not a compulsory requirement, depending on existing conditions." - §3.8 — "No practitioner may exclusively render professional services through Telehealth." This is the crucial bright line: a fully-remote practitioner is non-compliant. Sha'p Left is fine because the nurse is on-site; a Tier 4 doctor escalation supplements but does not replace the in-person nurse. - §5.1 — Competence, registration & authorisation: "Only practitioners who have been deemed competent and are registered in their respective professions are authorised to participate in Telehealth practice in South Africa, either as consulting healthcare practitioners or servicing healthcare practitioners. … In the case of Telehealth across country's borders, practitioner serving South African patients should be registered with the regulating bodies in their original states as well as with the HPCSA." Implication: any Tier 4 doctor in our solution must be HPCSA-registered. AU-registered doctors providing AU-Australia oversight to SA Sha'p Left clinics is non-compliant unless they also hold HPCSA registration. - §5.5 (b) — "Treatment, including issuing a prescription, based solely on an online questionnaire, does not constitute an acceptable standard of care." This rules out the LLM-only prescribe-without-clinical-judgment failure mode. - §5.7 — Informed consent for telehealth must include: identities + practice numbers, types of transmissions consented to, details of the security measures (encryption, password protection), material risks to confidentiality, secure storage, encryption of stored information, express consent to transmission of medical info to the practitioner or appropriate third parties. - §5.8 — Confidentiality: "Electronic transmissions, (e.g., email, prescriptions and laboratory results) must be secure within existing technology (e.g., password protected, encrypted electronic prescriptions or other reliable authentication techniques)." - §5.9.3 Security: "To protect the identity of the patient when information is transmitted between sites, it is essential that personal identification should be removed, and the transmitted information is encrypted." - §5.9.4 Safety: must comply with ECT Act 25/2002 + POPIA Act 4/2013 for data messages. - §6 — POPIA: "Practitioners are advised to ensure that they manage patients' information in accordance with requirements of POPIA."

Implications for a CDSS / ambient scribe: - The Tier 4 doctor-supervision pattern (Cipla MoU Article 2 scope) must be HPCSA-credential-verified at the practitioner level. Tshikululu MoU Article 18 implementation will need a "credential verification gate" in the on-call doctor sign-up path. - Telehealth informed-consent payload is richer than the AU equivalent: must include encryption details + material risks. Sophia's consent form needs an SA variant that names the encryption mechanism in plain language and lists the third-party processors (sub-processors per POPIA §20 + Tshikululu MoU Article 18.2). - "No exclusive telehealth practice" + "established practitioner-patient relationship preferred" = our Tier 4 escalation must be hand-off to a doctor who has prior knowledge of the patient OR escalated for the first time only with explicit consent. This is a workflow rule, not a tech rule; encode it as a banner + checkbox. - POPIA cross-border (§72): aligning with Tshikululu MoU Article 18.4 (SA-residency-by-default) and Article 18.8 (24-hour breach notification). Already on the roadmap.

5+8. NDOH Integrated Clinical Services Management (ICSM) Manual

What it is. The NDOH's flagship implementation manual for re-engineering PHC around an integrated patient-centred chronic + acute model. Published 2015 (the "Setting the scene" booklet) with a longer Manual updated through 2023 ("5th June FINAL", current public PDF on knowledgehub.health.gov.za 2023-04). 207 KB of plain text; covers facility-level patient flow, appointment scheduling, record integration, pre-dispensed medications, clinical algorithms (the PACK guide), chronic patient record, district clinical specialist team (DCST) supervision, ward-based outreach (WBPHCOT), school health team, point-of-care testing, high-risk screening, population health promotion, support groups.

Does it bind us? Operationally — every Sha'p Left clinic operates inside the ICSM model. The clinical algorithms used by SA PHC nurses are the PACK (Practical Approach to Care Kit) guides; the prescription patterns and chronic-disease management protocols ROSE recommends must trace back to PACK + PHC STG/EML 8th edition (2024). This was already flagged in concepts/shap-left-ambient-nurse-mvp-apr2026.md ("THE clinical reference: PHC STG/EML 8th edition (2024)"); ICSM is the system-level wrapper around STG/EML that defines how the encounter happens.

Key implications for the build: - Patient flow archetypes. ICSM defines four streams at every PHC facility: acute and minor ailments / MCWH preventive-promotive / chronic disease management / unplanned vs planned appointments. ROSE conversation flows must align to this taxonomy: the opening triage at a Sha'p Left clinic is "which of the 4 streams is this consult?" — same model used by Brandmed. - Headcount tooling — the ICSM Facility Data Tool tracks total headcount, <5y, >5y, then per-condition counts (Hypertension, Diabetes, Epilepsy, Asthma/COPD, Mental Health, ANC 1st booking, Well-baby Immunisation, Family Planning, Male Medical Circumcision, MOU Deliveries, etc.). For Tier 3 (population health surveillance) + the Cipla MoU Article 2.4 scope, this is the canonical reporting schema ROSE needs to roll up into. - Pre-dispensed medications + CCMDD pipeline — ICSM's chronic-stable-patient stream goes through the Central Chronic Medicine Dispensing & Distribution programme (= "Daplapmeds" in Sha'p Left's case). ROSE prescribing must support the "stable, divert to CCMDD/Daplapmeds" decision as a first-class workflow. - DCST supervision + WBPHCOT linkage — the ICSM model presumes a district clinical specialist team supervises facility-level care + ward-based outreach teams handle community follow-up. ROSE escalation logic should target the DCST for clinically complex cases, not just escalate to a remote doctor in Australia.

6. NDOH Ideal Clinic & Community Health Centre Facility Profile

What it is. A 13 KB structured form that every clinic and CHC in SA must capture annually on idealhealthfacility.org.za by the end of the first quarter of every financial year and update during the year as changes occur. Mandated by the OHSC Procedural Regulations (2016) under NHA §79(2)(b).

What it captures: - Facility identity (name, province, district, sub-district, ward, rural/urban, type) — auto-populated from DHIS facility data. - Contact details (manager, designation, address, postal, geo-coordinates, telephone, email, after-hours). - Health district + sub-district + ward + nearest reference point. - Operating days/hours, services offered (per category — chronic NCD, MCWH, HCT, family planning, etc.), bed/equipment counts. - Personnel breakdown by SANC category (PCNS / Professional Nurse / Enrolled Nurse / Enrolled Auxiliary).

Does it bind us? Sha'p Left clinics are private nurse-led PHC clinics — whether they are required to capture this profile is uncertain; the OHSC regulations primarily target public clinics + CHCs. Open question for Cipla (see §6 below).

Implications: - If Sha'p Left clinics are required to file the Facility Profile, ROSE/Sophia could auto-generate it from the platform's operational data — facility metadata + monthly headcount + service-mix. That's an immediate "value-add" for Cipla independently of CDSS. - Even if not legally required, the format defines the reporting schema that Cipla / DOH / Tshikululu will expect rollup into. Tier 3 (Population Health Surveillance) per the Cipla MoU should follow this schema.

7. Ideal Clinic Framework Version 19 (April 2022 update; Puni cites May 2021)

What it is. A 147 KB definitions + components + checklists framework for the Ideal Clinic Programme. Defines an "ideal clinic" as "a clinic with good infrastructure, adequate staff, adequate medicine and supplies, good administrative processes and sufficient bulk supplies, that uses applicable clinical policies, protocols, guidelines as well as partner and stakeholder support, to ensure the provision of quality health services to the community." 10 components × 33 sub-components. Components include: Administration / Integrated Clinical Services Management / Medicines, Supplies and Laboratory Services / Human Resources for Health / Support Services / Infrastructure / Health Information Management / Communication / District Health System Support / Implementing Partners and Stakeholders.

Does it bind us? Indirectly — it is the norms framework that the OHSC enforces via the Norms and Standards Regulations applicable to different categories of health establishments. Ideal Clinic status itself is voluntary, but the regulated norms are not. Text coloured green in the framework PDF marks the elements that are regulated under the Norms and Standards.

Implications: - Component 7 (Health Information Management) directly maps to ROSE / Sophia. Sophia must support facility-level Health Information Management in a way that meets the Ideal Clinic checklist criteria. The exact element list is in the Framework PDF (147 KB extracted to /home/adaptation/cipla-regs/Ideal-Clinic-Framework-V19.txt). Worth a dedicated read by the engineer building Tier 3. - Component 2 (Integrated Clinical Services Management) is the same as docs #5/#8 above — ROSE conversation flow alignment. - Component 3 (Medicines, Supplies and Laboratory Services) — the standardised facility-stationery list, EML stock visibility, NHLS lab integration — is informative for the prescribing-and-orders surface in Tier 1.

9. National Core Standards for Health Establishments in South Africa (2011)

What it is. The 87 KB foundational quality framework published by NDOH in 2011. Subtitle: "Towards Quality Care for Patients." The basis for the binding Norms and Standards Regulations applicable to different categories of health establishments (GG 1036 of 2018 + amendments) which the OHSC enforces. Seven Domains:

  1. Patient Rights — respect/dignity, access to information, physical access, continuity of care, reducing delays, emergency care, package of services, complaints management.
  2. Patient Safety, Clinical Governance and Clinical Care — covered next page after Domain 1 but truncated in our extract; covers IPC linkage, adverse event reporting, clinical guidelines compliance, prescribing safety, surgical safety etc.
  3. Clinical Support Services — pharmacy, lab, radiology, blood transfusion.
  4. Public Health — population health surveillance, communicable disease reporting, health promotion.
  5. Leadership and Governance — facility committee, M&E, financial governance, risk management.
  6. Operational Management — HR, finance, supply chain, transport.
  7. Facilities and Infrastructure — physical condition, security, waste management, equipment maintenance.

Does it bind us? Yes — every health establishment is measured against the seven domains. Cipla Foundation as the licence-holder for the SA medical-device establishment route (Cipla MoU Article 10) sits behind this; ROSE is a "clinical support service" that contributes to several of these domains.

Key verbatim (Domain 1 most directly affects Tier 1 design): - §1.1.1.2 — "Care provided maximises patient privacy." CDSS implication: ROSE must not display patient information on a screen visible to other patients; the nurse's screen needs orientation/privacy filter consideration. - §1.2.1.2 — "Procedures are followed to get informed consent." - §1.2.1.3 — "All patients receive a discharge report (as required by the National Health Act 61 of 2003)." CDSS implication: every Sha'p Left consult should produce a printable patient handout summarising assessment, plan, medications and follow-up — this is not just a UX nice-to-have but a regulatory requirement. - §1.4.1.1 — "An effective district health care referral policy and system is available, covering continuity of care, emergency care management, infrastructure and resources required." - §1.4.1.3 — "Patients are given information about referrals and specialist bookings." - §1.5.1.5 — "An efficient filing system is in place for patients' records." - §1.7.1.1 — "Services at the health establishment meet national guidelines or licensing specifications."

Implications for a CDSS: the patient handout / discharge note is mandatory output for every consult. Add this to Tier 1 MVP.

2. SANC PCNS — Section 56(6) deep dive

2.1 What the PCNS qualification is

The Primary Care Nurse Specialist (PCNS) is a SANC post-basic specialisation introduced in 2014 alongside the Generic Competency Framework for Advanced Nurse Practitioners, for the nurses who run PHC clinics — the cohort Sha'p Left employs. SANC's framing: "beyond a general nurse/midwife practitioner, there is need for the Advanced Practice Nurse within two levels, with the nurse specialist level requiring in-depth knowledge and expertise in a specific practice area and requiring a post-graduate diploma (PGD)". PCNS is the PHC-specific instance.

The 2014 PCNS competencies PDF is gated on sanc.co.za behind Cloudflare. The Cipla Drive copy (Puni's bundle attachment 12) remains canonical. What follows is built from R.2598 (scope), R.2418 (medicine schedules), the SANC Generic ANP framework, Crowley et al. 2024 (hsag.co.za/index.php/hsag/article/view/2582) and the Genoa 2025 explainer.

2.2 The two legal pathways for nurse prescribing

There are two distinct legal pathways under which a SA nurse can prescribe medicine, and both are load-bearing for ROSE:

Pathway A — Nursing Act §56(6) permit (state sector concession, the core PCNS pathway):

"Any nurse working in the state sector, or a designated organisation may be issued a permit allowing physical assessment, diagnosis of a 'physical defect, illness or deficiency', keeping, supplying, administering, and prescribing medicines." — Crowley et al. (2024) summarising §56(6) of the Nursing Act 33/2005

Critically the section explicitly notes that "such acts are intended to be performed by nurses only if the services of a medical practitioner or pharmacist are not available" and operates despite the Medicines Act 101/1965, the Pharmacy Act 53/1974 and the Health Professions Act 56/1974 — i.e., it is a parallel authorisation, not a delegation from a doctor.

Pathway B — Medicines Act §22A(15) permit (organisational permit for stocking/dispensing):

"the Director-General may, after consultation with the South African Pharmacy Council ... issue a permit to any person or organisation performing a health service, authorising such person or organisation to acquire, possess, use or supply any specified Schedule 1, Schedule 2, Schedule 3, Schedule 4 or Schedule 5 substance"

A nurse holding a §22A(15) permit is not an "authorised prescriber" and cannot issue prescriptions for dispensing at pharmacies. It lets nurses or organisations stock and supply — typical use is vaccines, contraception, family planning, home-based care. PCNS prescribing therefore comes from §56(6) plus R.2418 (below), not from §22A(15).

2.3 What schedules of medicines a PCNS can prescribe

The operative regulation is R.2418 of 1984 under the (then) Medicines Control Act, still in force: an authorised nurse may "keep, supply, administer or prescribe medicines containing substances listed in Schedule 0, Schedule 1, Schedule 2, Schedule 3 or Schedule 4." Schedules 5 and 6 are excluded — covers most antibiotics, antihypertensives, oral hypoglycaemics, contraceptives, ARVs (NIMART), TB regimens, antimalarials, basic analgesics, antenatal medicines; excludes opioid analgesia, strong psychotropics, controlled substances. Crowley et al. flag this as "a stumbling block for the psychiatric nurse and the nurse in palliative care". Operational rule for ROSE: PHC STG/EML 8th edition (2024) is the de facto nurse formulary — anything outside it must not appear in PCNS recommendations.

2.4 Conditions a PCNS may diagnose and manage independently

No statutory list exists. §56(6) permits "physical assessment, diagnosis of a 'physical defect, illness or deficiency'" — Crowley et al. note this excludes mental-health diagnosis. Operational lists come from PHC STG/EML 2024 + Adult Primary Care (APC) + IMCI. PCNS-independent conditions: hypertension (init/titrate within target; escalate ≥180/110, end-organ damage, pregnancy), T2DM (metformin + SU; insulin → GP), asthma/COPD (salbutamol + ICS; severe → escalate), HIV under NIMART (TLD first-line; 2nd-line + paediatric init → GP), DS-TB, STI syndromic, antenatal BANC+, family planning + EC, common acute presentations (URTI, gastroenteritis, soft-tissue, headache, wounds).

2.5 Mandatory escalation triggers (production rule set)

PCNS must escalate when: (1) condition outside PHC STG/APC algorithms; (2) recommended medicine is Schedule 5 or 6; (3) investigation not performable at clinic (CT, advanced ECG, advanced bloods); (4) IMCI/APC red flag fires (severe paediatric pneumonia, eclampsia, sepsis, suspected MI/stroke); (5) minor under 12 without parent/guardian (Children's Act §129); (6) mental-health diagnosis (excluded from §56(6) "physical" wording). These belong in the ROSE rules engine as a hard gate.

2.6 The SANC authorisation gap

§22A(14) requires SANC to formally authorise nurses as prescribers. Crowley et al.: "currently, there is a notable absence of SANC guidelines or regulations overseeing the authorisation process for nurses to diagnose and prescribe". Prescribing happens daily in PHC clinics under §56(6) + R.2418 + PHC STG, but the SANC competency-register architecture is incomplete. ROSE should rely on employer-issued §56(6) permit + clinic-level §22A(15) permit + PHC-STG-bounded recommendations — not per-nurse SANC lookups.

3. POPIA — requirements relevant to a CDSS

POPIA = Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013, in force from 1 July 2021. Health information is special personal information under §26 — banned from processing unless a §27 exception applies (clinical-care ones do).

§19 Security safeguards — "A responsible party must secure the integrity and confidentiality of personal information ... by taking appropriate, reasonable technical and organisational measures to prevent (a) loss of, damage to or unauthorised destruction of personal information; and (b) unlawful access to or processing of personal information." §19(2): identify foreseeable risks, maintain + verify safeguards, update against new risks. For ROSE: encryption at rest + in transit, RBAC, audit logging, periodic pen-testing, documented ISMS. ISO 27001 not legally required but is the industry baseline; Tshikululu MoU v2.2 Article 18.5 references AES-256 + FIPS-grade KMS.

§21 Operator obligations — operator (cloud / sub-processor / AI vendor) must process only with responsible party's authority, treat data as confidential, maintain §19-equivalent security, be bound by written contract, and notify "immediately" on suspected breach. Botaniqal = operator for Cipla Foundation NPC (responsible party); Tshikululu = sub-processor under MoU Art 18.2.

§22 Breach notification — "Where there are reasonable grounds to believe that the personal information of a data subject has been accessed or acquired by any unauthorised person, the responsible party must notify (a) the Regulator; and (b) ... the data subject." Statutory deadline: "as soon as reasonably possible". Tshikululu MoU Art 18.8 contractually shortens to 24 hours — operational deadline.

§26-27 Special personal information (health) — banned unless exception applies. Relevant: §27(1)(a) data-subject consent; §32(1) processing by registered health professional/institution for treatment + care. Both available at Sha'p Left; consent remains primary basis.

§71 Automated decision-making — prohibits decisions taken "solely on the basis of the automated processing of personal information intended to provide a profile" with legal effect, unless appropriate measures including right to make representations. ROSE recommendations must always be advisory: clinician-in-the-loop non-negotiable. PCNS, not ROSE, is decision-maker on the record.

§72 Trans-border data flow (AU ↔︎ SA) — export permitted only if: (a) recipient subject to law/BCRs/binding agreement giving "an adequate level of protection" with principles "substantially similar" to POPIA, (b) specific voluntary informed consent, (c) contract performance with data subject, (d) third-party contract in data subject's interest, or (e) data-subject benefit + impractical consent. Production target = (a): AU Privacy Act 1988 + APPs accepted as substantially similar; binding agreement between AU parent + SA operator. Tshikululu MoU Art 18.4 default = SA-residency-by-default (eventual af-south-1 migration), at which point §72 stops biting.

§99 Administrative fines — up to ZAR 10,000,000 per offence (or imprisonment up to 10 years for §107 offences). This is the penalty Article 18 of the Tshikululu MoU is sized against.

4. NHA / MCA / Nursing Act / ECTA / RICA / PAIA / Children's / CTOP / MHCA

National Health Act 61/2003. §13 (confidentiality): "All information concerning a user, including information relating to his or her health status, treatment or stay in a health establishment, is confidential." Disclosure unlawful unless (a) written user consent, (b) court order / law, (c) non-disclosure = serious public-health threat. §14 (user responsibilities — accurate info, discharge/refusal sign-off). §15 (access by health workers — disclosure permitted within treating-circle "for any legitimate purpose within the ordinary course and scope of their duties where such access or disclosure is in the interests of the user"; §13 still applies outside that circle). §17 (head of facility duty to prevent unauthorised access — mirrors POPIA §19). §35-39 (facility tiers — drives ROSE referral logic, see Cipla bundle doc #2). §61 (discharge report — informs ROSE end-of-encounter summary).

Medicines and Related Substances Act 101/1965 — §22A. Schedules: 0 (general sales), 1-2 (pharmacist-supplied), 3-4 (prescription), 5 (benzodiazepines, weak opioids), 6 (morphine + strong controlled), 7-8 (research/restricted). PCNS scope under R.2418 = Schedules 0-4. §22A(15) = organisational stocking permit. §22C = SAHPRA establishment licence for SaMD manufacturers/importers (relevant to Botaniqal — see §5).

Nursing Act 33/2005. Covered above. §30 defines scope (further specified by R.2598/1984); §58-59 grant ministerial regulation-making power. PCNS sits at SANC's "Advanced Practice Nurse — Specialist" tier.

ECT Act 25/2002 — electronic transactions + signatures. §13: where "the signature of a person is required by law and such law does not specify the type of signature, that requirement in relation to a data message is met only if an advanced electronic signature is used." If NHA §13 requires written consent, ECTA §13 forces an advanced electronic signature (AES) created by an accredited authentication-service-provider under §37. §14-15: data messages = writing. Rule: support AES on consent + prescription artefacts, otherwise wet signature on paper. §43 consumer-protection: not load-bearing for the Cipla pilot.

RICA Act 70/2002 — interception + ambient recording. Exceptions relevant to ROSE ambient capture: §4 (party-to-communication — clinician is a party), §5 (prior written consent of any one party), §6 (course of business — brittle). Rule: ambient-scribe consent must be (a) written (paper or AES), (b) specific (names ROSE/Sophia, recording, retention), (c) voluntary (refusable without service denial), (d) informed (data flows, AU hosting, retention). One consent text satisfies POPIA §11, NHA §13, RICA §5 simultaneously.

PAIA Act 2/2000 — patient access requests. Constitutional §32 right; procedural mechanism = PAIA Form 2 (private body) / Form A (public). Botaniqal must (i) publish a PAIA Manual, (ii) designate an Information Officer (POPIA §55 dual role), (iii) route patient requests via Cipla / Sha'p Left clinic to Botaniqal as operator.

Children's Act 38/2005 §129. "A child may consent to his or her own medical treatment ... provided that the child is — (a) over the age of 12 years; and (b) of sufficient maturity and has the mental capacity to understand the benefits, risks, social and other implications of the treatment." Surgical procedures: 12+, mature, and parent/guardian-assisted. "Sufficient maturity" undefined — clinician judgement. Rule for ROSE: < 12 → surface parent/guardian consent block; 12-17 → surface §129 maturity-assessment and require PCNS to record positive determination. §130 HIV testing: age 12, no surgical/maturity overlay — relevant to HCT flow.

CTOP Act 92/1996. "No consent other than that of the pregnant woman shall be required for the termination of a pregnancy." Practitioner / registered midwife must advise a pregnant minor to consult parent / guardian / family / friend — but cannot withhold termination if she declines. Confidentiality reinforced by Constitution + NHA §13. Tension with Criminal Law (Sexual Offences) Amendment Act mandatory-reporting where minor: ROSE must surface the conflict, not auto-disclose — clinician judgement.

Mental Health Care Act 17/2002. Governs confidentiality + involuntary admission (Forms MHCA 04, 05, 22 under §17-23). §56(6) Nursing Act explicitly excludes mental-health diagnosis; PCNS must escalate any positive PHQ-9 / suspected psychosis / acute risk to a medical practitioner. ROSE must never produce a final mental-health diagnosis as a PCNS-facing recommendation — flag screening positives + recommend referral only.

5. SAHPRA SaMD framework (likely classification of ROSE)

5.1 The framework documents

  1. SAHPGL-MD-04 v5 (27 Feb 2025) — Guideline for Classification of Medical Devices and IVDs. Four-class system (A/B/C/D) + Rules 1-16 + IVD rules.
  2. MD08-2025/2026 (26 Sept 2025) — Regulatory Requirements of AI/ML Enabled Medical Devices. First formal SAHPRA position on AI/ML SaMD.
  3. Regulation 1515 / GG 40480 (9 Dec 2016) — General regulations for medical devices including IVDs (parent regulation).

5.2 The classification system

Verbatim from SAHPGL-MD-04 v5: "Class A — Low risk / Class B — Low–moderate risk / Class C — Moderate–high risk / Class D — High risk — where risk relates to the patient or to public health". IMDRF-aligned: ~95 % portability if classified for FDA / EU MDR / TGA. Rules that matter for ROSE: Rule 10 (active diagnostic), Rule 12 (active general), IMDRF SaMD framework (called up by MD08).

5.3 Where ROSE / Sophia lands

MD08 is unambiguous about CDSS:

"This includes but is not limited to AI/ML applications in: ... Clinical decision support systems (e.g., AI-driven diagnostic aids or treatment recommendation systems for healthcare professionals) ..."

"In general, an AI software that is intended to drive or influence clinical decisions directly (and where such decisions could result in significant patient harm if incorrect) will likely fall into Class C or D (higher risk)."

ROSE Tier 1 CDSS at Sha'p Left = Class C SaMD minimum, possibly Class D. Influences PCNS decisions on R.2418-schedule prescribing + escalation; incorrect output could cause patient harm (wrong dose, missed escalation, missed red flag). Tier 4 Supervising Prescribing Authority (Cipla MoU 4-tier scope) = almost certainly Class D.

5.4 What "regulated" means today — and the timing

"While SAHPRA has not commenced with product registration of medical devices and IVDs as per regulation 8 of the general regulations for medical devices including IVDs (Regulation No. 1515 ... 9 December 2016), the rapid advancement and adoption of such technologies necessitate proactive engagement ..."

"any person or company intending to import, manufacture and market such a product in South Africa shall apply to the Authority for a medical device establishment licence issued under Section 22C of the Medicines and Related Substances Act 101 of 1965 ..."

Takeaway: SAHPRA has not yet "called up" device registration — ROSE doesn't need full SAHPRA registration today. But Botaniqal does need a §22C establishment licence + must list ROSE on it. When SAHPRA calls up the class (expected within 2025-2030 strategic plan), full registration becomes required: IMDRF-aligned technical file, QMS to ISO 13485, software lifecycle to IEC 62304, risk to ISO 14971.

5.5 The five MD08 guiding principles (active today regardless of registration status)

  1. Patient safety + risk management — software-engineering rigour, failure-mode analysis, human-in-the-loop fallback. (2) Transparency + explainability — document how AI decides, intended use, limits, uncertainty; users get rationale for recommendations (Sophia's quote-grounding maps cleanly). (3) Cybersecurity + data integrity + privacy — POPIA compliance, pseudonymisation, informed consent. (4) Performance monitoring + adaptability — drift detection, controls on continuous learning, safety-preserving updates. (5) Clinical evaluation + performance — independent test datasets, subgroup analyses (sex/age/ethnicity), real-world metrics.

ROSE's benchmark testing process (Goal A 4-Claude Byzantine consensus + Goal B citation-disciplined errata + Goal C category-iterating fixes) answers (1), (2), (4), (5). Cybersecurity (3) needs explicit SOC 2 / ISO 27001 trajectory (mirrors Tshikululu MoU 18.5).

7. Notifiable Medical Conditions list + reporting

Legal basis: NHA: Regulations relating to the Surveillance and Control of Notifiable Medical Conditions, GN R.1434 of 15 Dec 2017; disease list updated by NICD (most recent Dec 2024).

Categories + timelines (NICD four-category system): - Category 1 — immediate by rapid means (phone / NMC app), then written within 24 h. Examples (Dec 2024): Anthrax, AFP, Cholera, CCHF, Diphtheria, Ebola/Marburg, EHEC, Food-borne outbreak, Hib, Measles, Meningococcal, Mpox, Plague, Polio, Rabies (human), Novel respiratory pathogen, Rift Valley fever, Smallpox, Typhoid, VHF, Yellow fever. - Category 2 — written/electronic within 7 days. Examples: most TB forms, congenital rubella, hepatitis A/B/C, lead/mercury poisoning, legionellosis, listeriosis, malaria, MDR/XDR-TB, paratyphoid, pertussis, salmonellosis, shigellosis, tetanus, brucellosis, leprosy. - Category 3 — monthly aggregate surveillance. - Category 4 — laboratory-only confirmed (HIV goes through DHIS2 / TIER.Net, not NMC).

Reporting mechanism: canonical = NMC mobile app (2017+, Android/iOS) → NICD electronic NMC database. Fallbacks: GW17/5 paper Notification Form → local CDC Co-ordinator + provincial DoH; email to NICD Notifiable Conditions desk.

Rule for ROSE: when a Category 1 condition is on the differential, surface a reminder banner ("Category 1 NMC — notify within 24 h via NMC app or CDC Coordinator"). ROSE must not auto-submit — duty is on clinician + facility, not the SaMD vendor.
